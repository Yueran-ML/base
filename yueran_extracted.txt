                  A Three-Stage Picture of Grokking:
 Circuit Formation, Generalization, and Embedding Geometry

                                Consolidation

                                              Anonymous Authors

                                                                         Abstract

             We study temporal dynamics during transformer grokking on modular arithmetic, directly measuring three distinct
        milestones in the same training runs: Fourier circuit formation onset circuit (logit-level), generalization onset gen
        (test accuracy), and embedding-geometry consolidation F (token embedding Fourier alignment). We provide direct
        empirical evidence for the full three-stage ordering circuit < gen < F : the Fourier computation circuit forms
        before generalization, which in turn precedes the convergence of token embedding geometry (C<G<F in 48/54
        Grokking runs, 88.9%).

             Specifically, logit Fourier alignment shows circuit formation precedes generalization in 50/54 runs (92.6%, median
        gap 6,250 steps), while embedding geometry lags generalization in 52/55 runs (94.5%, median lag  7,000 steps),
        confirming the G<F ordering of Nanda et al.'s "cleanup" phase interpretation. We extend the G<F result across
        four experimental variations: a dense 7×7 2D (lr×wd) grid (111/130 = 85.4%), a different prime p=97 (21/21 =
        100%, median lag 28,000 steps), and modular multiplication via discrete-log reindexing (30/30 = 100%). Together
        these results establish that embedding-level Fourier geometry is a post-generalization structural consequence--not a
        prerequisite--across tasks, primes, and hyperparameter regimes.

1 Introduction

Grokking--the abrupt onset of generalization long after training accuracy saturates--has become a canonical testbed
for studying delayed representation learning in neural networks [Power et al., 2022]. On modular arithmetic tasks,
Nanda et al. [2023] showed that grokked transformers implement a Fourier circuit: the network uses trigonometric
identities over a small set of task-relevant harmonics to compute modular addition. As a consequence, the p token
embeddings form a near-circular ring in PCA space, with embedding i at angle 2i/p.

    Nanda et al. [2023] also introduced mechanism-level progress measures--quantities such as the Fourier-squared
loss--and found that these measures improve during a circuit-formation phase that precedes the generalization jump.
This raises a natural question about a related but distinct quantity: the token embedding geometry itself. Does
embedding-level Fourier alignment also consolidate before generalization, or does it lag behind?

    Answering this requires measuring the relative timing of two events:

     · gen: generalization onset--the first checkpoint where test accuracy  0.9 and remains there for three consecutive
       evaluations;

     · F : embedding-geometry onset--the step at which token embeddings first show significantly Fourier-aligned
       structure above a permutation-null baseline (BIC changepoint on null-corrected Fourier R2).

    Prior work has not directly measured  = F - gen. Nanda et al. [2023] studied mechanism-level dynamics
but did not separately track embedding geometry. Liu et al. [2022] built a static phase diagram but did not measure
within-run event timing. Our work closes this gap.

    Our results establish a three-stage picture of grokking: (1) Fourier circuit formation circuit, (2) generalization
onset gen, (3) embedding-geometry consolidation F . All three timescales are measured directly in the same runs. The
familiar Fourier ring visible in PCA visualizations reflects stage (3)--a post-generalization structural consequence--
rather than stage (1), which drives the generalization jump.

2 Background

2.1 Grokking and the Phase Diagram

Power et al. [2022] documented grokking in small transformers on binary operations including (a + b) mod p. Liu et al.
[2022] mapped training behavior across a (decoder learning rate, weight decay) grid, identifying four phases: Grokking
(delayed generalization), Memorization (no generalization), Comprehension (immediate generalization), and Confusion
(no learning). Our experiments follow their architecture and phase conventions.

2.2 Fourier Circuits and Embedding Geometry

Nanda et al. [2023] reverse-engineered the algorithm underlying grokked transformers: the network computes modular
sums via trigonometric identities over task-relevant Fourier harmonics. They introduced mechanism-level progress
measures and found that circuit formation--the process by which the model constructs the Fourier computation--
precedes the test-accuracy jump, followed by a "cleanup" phase in which memorization components are removed. Their
analysis focused on the computational mechanism (logit-level Fourier structure and progress measures); we study both
that mechanism and a complementary quantity: the geometric organization of the token embeddings themselves. We
introduce a logit Fourier alignment score FL that measures circuit-level structure (how much logit variance is explained
by Fourier harmonics of the answer class), and compare its onset circuit with embedding-geometry onset F in the
same runs. Progress measures / FL track how much of the Fourier computation is already in place; Fcorr tracks when
the token embedding table has converged to its final geometry.

3 Related Work

Grokking and delayed generalization. Power et al. [2022] introduced grokking on modular arithmetic. Liu et al.
[2022] constructed the (lr, wd) phase diagram we use as our experimental scaffold, identifying the four training phases
we adopt. Liu et al. [2023] showed that grokking extends well beyond algorithmic tasks and is governed by the ratio of
initial weight norm to the norm of the generalising solution. Thilak et al. [2022] linked grokking to cyclic instabilities
of adaptive optimisers, terming this the "slingshot mechanism." On the theoretical side, Rubin et al. [2024] model
grokking as a first-order phase transition, while Lyu et al. [2024] prove that a dichotomy of early- and late-phase implicit
biases can provably induce grokking in homogeneous networks. These works characterise when generalization occurs;
our work characterises its timing relative to embedding geometry and circuit formation.

learn group operations more broadly, finding that the learned circuits depend on the group structure in a predictable
way. Zhong et al. [2023] show that modular addition admits two distinct algorithms (the "Clock" and "Pizza" variants),
depending on initialisation and architecture, revealing that the Fourier circuit is not the unique generalising solution.
Gromov [2023] provides analytic weight solutions for two-layer networks on modular arithmetic tasks, establishing
complete interpretability of the learned representations. Conmy et al. [2023] automate circuit discovery via activation
patching, complementing our temporal approach to identifying when circuits form. Our Fcorr metric is complementary
to all of the above: it tracks when the token embedding table reaches its final geometric arrangement, which lags
generalization by thousands of steps in most configurations.

Embedding geometry and representation dynamics. Saxe et al. [2014] showed that learning in deep linear networks
proceeds in discrete stages corresponding to singular value decomposition, an early theoretical account of structured
representation formation. Papyan et al. [2020] identified "Neural Collapse," the terminal convergence of within-class
activations to a simplex equiangular tight frame, as a generic post-convergence geometric phenomenon in classification
networks. Our F result is analogous: the Fourier ring geometry of token embeddings is also a post-generalization
geometric consolidation, not a prerequisite for generalization. Elhage et al. [2022] show that polysemanticity and
superposition are widespread in transformer features; our focus on the token embedding's one-dimensional ring structure
provides a concrete, measurable geometric ground truth against which timing can be assessed. Barak et al. [2022]
demonstrate that abrupt generalization can hide gradual progress in loss-scale metrics, motivating our BIC changepoint
approach to detect latent structural transitions.

Methods. We use AdamW [Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019] and adopt the transformer architecture of Vaswani et al.
[2017] as extended to the circuit framework of Elhage et al. [2021]. Our changepoint estimator is based on the Bayesian
Information Criterion [Schwarz, 1978].

4 Methods

4.1 Task and Model

We train a decoder-only transformer [Vaswani et al., 2017] on (a + b) mod 53. All p2 = 2,809 input pairs are
generated; 30% (843 pairs) form the training set. The model uses dmodel = 256, 4 attention heads, 2 layers, feed-
forward dimension 1024, no dropout. The embedding layer has fixed learning rate emb = 10-3 and weight decay 0;
only decoder parameters are swept. We use AdamW [Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019] with a 10-step linear warmup
followed by constant learning rate, and gradient clipping at norm 1.0.

6 Discussion

6.1 Embedding Geometry as a Lagging Indicator

Our results are compatible with a picture of grokking that distinguishes mechanism formation from embedding
consolidation. Nanda et al. [2023] showed that the Fourier computation circuit forms--and progress measures improve--
before the generalization jump gen. Our complementary finding is that the token embedding geometry (measured by
Fcorr) continues to consolidate for thousands of steps after gen.

                                      7
    Our results directly support a three-stage picture: (1) Fourier circuit formation circuit (this work, via logit Fourier
alignment); (2) generalization onset gen; (3) embedding-geometry consolidation F (this work). All three timescales
are measured in the same runs, without relying on Nanda et al.'s separate dataset. The three intervals are approximately
equal: median gen - circuit  F - gen  6,000­6,250 steps, so the total span F - circuit  13,000 steps is
split nearly evenly between stages (12) and (23). The familiar Fourier ring visible in PCA visualizations reflects

stage (3)--a post-generalization structural consequence--not stage (1), which drives the generalization jump.

    A further finding is consistent with this view: F_only runs (Fourier geometry detected but no generalization) exist at
lr  5.9 × 10-3, showing that Fourier geometric organization is insufficient for generalization near the phase boundary.
Together, G<F and F_only runs suggest that this measured Fcorr-based embedding alignment is neither synchronized
with generalization (it lags gen in the G<F regime) nor predictive of it (Fourier structure can accumulate without
triggering generalization in the F_only regime). Whether this implies anything about the underlying Fourier circuit
mechanism--which we do not directly measure--requires further investigation with circuit-level probes.

6.2 Why the Metric Matters

We use null-corrected Fcorr rather than raw Fraw because random embeddings already exhibit non-trivial Fraw in high
dimensions (p = 53, d = 256): any projection of 53 points in 256 dimensions partially aligns with some harmonic by
chance. The null-corrected Fcorr removes this baseline and marks the step at which embeddings show significantly
above-chance harmonic structure.

    To assess whether null correction changes the conclusions of this paper, we applied the BIC changepoint estimator
independently to Fcorr and Fraw for all 8 sensitivity cells (Table 5). For cells with | |  3,000 steps, the two metrics
agree: 6 of 6 G<F cells remain G<F under raw Fourier, because the sharp post-gen rise in Fraw dominates the
changepoint detection regardless of baseline. The null correction matters most for marginal cases: the coincident cell
(wd= 3.11) and the F<G outlier remain unchanged. We conclude that null correction does not alter the main finding
for runs with substantial lags, but is conceptually important for calibrating the detection threshold and avoiding false
positives in the coincident regime.

6.3 Detector Robustness

A natural concern is whether the G<F finding depends on the specific choice of detector hyperparameters: the null-
update interval, the EMA smoothing coefficient, and the BIC slope threshold. To address this, we trained 8 representative
cells spanning strong, medium, and weak G<F cases, one coincident case, and the sole stable F<G case, and applied
all 3 × 4 × 3 = 36 detector configurations post-hoc to the same saved trajectories.

    We define the Robustness Score RS = NG<F /Ntotal for each cell, where Ntotal = 36. Results are shown in
Figure 10. The five cells with | |  5,000 steps achieve RS = 1.00 (36/36 configurations yield G<F). The two cells
with | |  2,000­3,000 steps achieve RS= 0.83 and RS= 0.75, with the remaining configurations classifying as
coincident rather than F<G. Crucially, no G<F cell is ever classified as F<G under any configuration. The mean RS
across all seven non-F<G cells is 0.94, confirming that the G<F finding forms a robustness plateau over the detector
parameter space.

before the sharp gen transition. Whether this reflects a qualitatively different mechanism or an extreme-parameter-ratio

    lr= 4.32×10-3, seed 42 ( = -54,500, RS not assessed). This is the most extreme case: F = 13,000 but
gen = 67,500 (extended to 80k steps), with max_test_acc= 0.965 (below 1.0, indicating incomplete generalization).
This run sits near the Grokking/Memorization phase boundary. We conjecture that at high lr, the model can develop

    Both cases occur near the boundaries of the Grokking phase (low-lr edge and high-lr edge), suggesting the G<F

6.4 Limitations

Task scope. Our three-stage confirmation covers (a + b) mod p and (a · b) mod 53. Stage 5B extends to p=97.
Generalization to non-modular tasks, deeper architectures, or non-algorithmic settings remains open.

Measurement resolution.  is measured with 500-step resolution; small lags (| |  500) cannot be reliably
classified. One F<G run has | | = 1,500 (three log-intervals), above the resolution floor but warranting caution.

Logit metric alignment. Our FL captures the aggregate Fourier structure of logits across all (a, b) pairs, which
is related but not identical to Nanda et al.'s restricted logit loss. Whether the two metrics detect exactly the same
circuit-formation event or slightly different aspects of it remains to be verified by direct comparison on the same runs.

Phase-boundary regime. The 4 G<C<F exceptions in Stage 4 all occur at high lr where generalization is very fast.
The BIC changepoint may underestimate circuit when the logit signal rises gradually rather than sharply, so these
exceptions may partly reflect detection limits rather than genuine G<C ordering.

Phase-boundary regime and task-specificity. Both F<G runs in our addition sweeps occur at the margins of
the Grokking phase where gen is unusually late (43,500 and 67,500 steps). A pilot run of modular subtraction
((a-b) mod 53, wd= 2.18) further supports this pattern: subtraction groks much later than addition at the same
hyperparameters (gen=43,500 vs  11,000 for addition), and the ordering is F<G ( =-9,000). Together, these
observations suggest a speed-dependent ordering hypothesis: G<F holds when generalization is fast (small gen);
when grokking is delayed--whether by extreme hyperparameters or by a harder task--weight-decay-driven embedding
consolidation can precede generalization, yielding F<G. Formally testing this hypothesis across a wider task and
hyperparameter space is an important open direction. Figure 11 provides preliminary quantitative support: across 57
Grokking runs (55 addition + 2 subtraction pilots),  correlates negatively with gen (r = -0.62); all F<G runs have
gen  43,000 steps, whereas the majority of G<F runs have gen  35,000 steps. Crucially, two subtraction pilots
at the same hyperparameters (wd= 2.18, lr= 1.6×10-3) provide within-task evidence: seed 42 (gen=43,500, slow)
gives F<G ( = - 9,000), while seed 7 (gen=27,500, faster) gives G<F ( = + 5,500)--the same hyperparameters
produce opposite orderings depending solely on how quickly the run generalizes. We note that  = F - gen is partly
mathematically related to gen, so the aggregate correlation partly reflects this construction; however, the within-task
seed comparison is free of this confound and directly supports the speed-dependent interpretation.

7 Conclusion

We have directly measured all three stages of a three-stage picture of grokking in the same training runs: Fourier circuit
formation circuit, generalization onset gen, and embedding-geometry consolidation F . The full C<G<F ordering
holds in 48/54 Grokking runs (88.9%), with approximately equal gaps between consecutive stages ( 6,000­6,250 steps
each).

    Across 236 Grokking runs spanning two tasks, two primes, and a dense 2D hyperparameter grid, 89.8% show
G<F ordering. The G<F rate reaches 100% on 1D hyperparameter slices (Stages 2, 5A, 5B) and 85.4% on the full
2D grid (Stage 6), with exceptions concentrated at phase boundaries where grokking is abnormally slow. Median
embedding-geometry lag ranges from  6,000 steps (p=53) to 28,000 steps (p=97), scaling with the difficulty of the
task.

    The familiar Fourier ring in grokking PCA visualizations reflects the third stage--a post-generalization structural
consequence--not the first (circuit formation), which drives the generalization jump.

    A methodological takeaway: raw Fourier alignment can shift the estimated onset earlier, but in our sensitivity
cells it does not reverse the ordering sign (0/8 flips; Table 5). Null correction is nonetheless important for principled
calibration, as it prevents false positives near the coincident boundary where Fraw reflects high-dimensional chance
alignment rather than genuine Fourier structure.

Broader impact. This work studies a small-scale neural network training phenomenon. No immediate harmful
applications are foreseen. Findings may inform training efficiency research by clarifying which structural changes are
or are not necessary for generalization.

A Experimental Details

A.1 BIC Changepoint Estimator

We apply an exponential moving average (=0.15) to the Fcorr trajectory, then operate on log(1 + t). We search all
candidate breakpoints in the interior two-thirds of the sequence, fitting OLS regressions before and after each candidate.
The 4-parameter breakpoint model is accepted if BICbreak < BICno-break, where BICk = n log(RSS/n) + k log(n). A
persistence check requires the post-break slope to exceed 1% of the total value range.

A.2 Null Permutation Estimation

Each run maintains a fixed NumPy RNG seeded as run_seed  0xDEAD, independent of the model RNG. We shuffle
the p token indices 100 times every 5,000 steps to estimate Fnull,p95.
